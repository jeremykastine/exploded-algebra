Introduction to Exploded Algebra

1. Why grouping must be clear

The phrase “two plus three times four” can be said, interpreted, and evaluated in two different ways.

If one says “two … plus three times four” (pausing after “two”), then that pause suggests that “three times four” belongs together: multiply 3 by 4 to get 12, then add 2. The result is 14.

If one says “two plus three … times four” (pausing after “three”), then that pause suggests that “two plus three” belongs together: add 2 and 3 to get 5, then multiply that entire result by 4. The result is 20. The same words can point toward two different groupings—and two different answers.

In mathematics, we need a concise and unambiguous way to represent these calculations. That is what a mathematical expression is meant to provide: a description of a calculation, often involving several steps, in a concise arrangement of symbols. To communicate the intended calculation accurately, that arrangement must make clear which parts belong together.

Conventional mathematical notation and the English language have each developed their own ways of communicating grouping more clearly than in the phrase discussed above. For now, let’s set those familiar conventions aside and allow ourselves to experience a new way of visualizing structure and grouping within an expression.

2. Exploded-view diagrams

An exploded-view diagram shows the parts of an assembled object separated in space. Pulling the parts apart in the picture makes it easier to see the individual components and how they fit together.

The parts are not scattered at random. Their relative positions help us see their relationships: which pieces fit inside others, which pieces sit beside one another, and which pieces combine to form a larger whole. Here are three examples.

Exploded Algebra borrows this idea for mathematical expressions. We use spatial separation to help visualize grouping: which pieces belong together in one operation, and how that whole group fits into a larger calculation. A group can be seen as one part of an expression while its own smaller parts remain visible.

The calculation itself does not change. We are changing how we arrange its symbols so that its structure is easier to see. Let’s return to “two plus three times four” and see what this approach looks like.

3. A fresh way to draw an expression

In Exploded Algebra, the terms of a sum are arranged vertically. A plus sign is centered between each pair of adjacent terms, with a horizontal bar extending to its left and right. Each bar spans the width of the widest term, visually grouping all the terms that belong to the sum.

The factors of a product are arranged horizontally. A multiplication sign is centered between each pair of adjacent factors, with a vertical bar extending above and below it. Each bar spans the height of the tallest factor, visually grouping all the factors that belong to the product. These bars are present even when every term or factor is a single number.

The ellipses below mark the same pauses we used in Section 1. In the first picture, the whole sum of 2 and 3 is a factor beside 4, giving 20. In the second, the whole product of 3 and 4 is a term below 2, giving 14. The grouping segments show which operation contains the other.

6. Algebraic properties with numbers

We can sometimes make a calculation easier by changing the arrangement of an expression while keeping its value the same. The commutative properties and the distributive property tell us which changes we can make. Let’s look at numerical examples, using English and exploded notation.

The commutative property of addition lets us switch the order of two terms. Adding 2 to 3 gives the same total as adding 3 to 2. Imagine combining a group of two objects with a group of three objects: changing which group we count first does not change the total of five.

“Add 2 and 3.”

Two is above three in the sum, with a horizontal addition bar between them.

The total is 5.

“Switch their order: add 3 and 2.”

Three is now above two. The addition bar still groups the same two terms.

The total is still 5.

The commutative property of multiplication lets us switch the order of two factors. Three groups of four objects contain the same number of objects as four groups of three. Picture three rows with four objects in each row, then turn the picture to see four rows with three in each. Both arrangements contain twelve objects.

“Multiply 3 by 4.”

Three and four sit side by side, with a vertical multiplication bar between them.

The product is 12.

“Switch their order: multiply 4 by 3.”

Four is now to the left of three. Both are still in the same product row.

The product is still 12.

The distributive property lets us multiply a sum by a number by multiplying each term by that number and adding the resulting products. For example, adding 3 and 4 and then doubling the sum gives fourteen. Doubling means taking two copies of everything in the sum: two copies of 3 and two copies of 4. Those products are six and eight, whose sum is also fourteen.

“Add 3 and 4, then multiply the sum by 2.”

The vertical sum of three and four sits to the right of a single factor of two.

The sum is 7; doubling it gives 14.

“Multiply 2 by 3 and 2 by 4, then add the products.”

Two horizontal product rows are stacked as terms of a sum. Each row begins with its own factor of two.

The products are 6 and 8; their sum is 14.

We can also use the distributive property in reverse, which is called factoring. If we are adding two products that share a factor, we can take that shared factor outside a sum of the remaining factors. In the example below, both products contain 2. Adding two copies of 3 to two copies of 4 is the same as taking two copies of their combined amount. We can therefore add 3 and 4 first and then double the result.

“Add the product of 2 and 3 to the product of 2 and 4.”

A stack of two product rows shows the same leading factor of two in each row.

The total is 14.

“Factor out the shared 2: add 3 and 4, then multiply by 2.”

The two copies of the common factor have been replaced by one factor beside a vertical sum.

The total is still 14.

Each pair describes the same value in two different arrangements. The exploded diagrams show the complete terms or factors being switched, distributed, or factored, and make the resulting grouping explicit.

Bring the two products containing 35 together and put 35 first in each product. Factoring out 35 leaves a sum of 43 and 7, which is 50. Both terms now share a factor of 50. Factoring again leaves a sum of 35 and 65, which is 100. The expression is now a product of 2, 100, and 50.

The rearranged route uses more structural moves, but much simpler arithmetic. The algebraic properties preserve the value while letting us choose a more convenient way to calculate it. Throughout both routes, the grouping remains explicit.

9. Grouping in conventional and exploded notation

Let’s return to the two calculations from Section 3. In conventional notation, parentheses can explicitly specify the hierarchy: the calculation inside a pair of parentheses forms one complete part of the surrounding calculation. Here every operation is enclosed in parentheses.

two plus three … times four

One pair of parentheses marks the sum of two and three. Another encloses the whole calculation, including multiplication by four.

The inner sum is evaluated first. The result is 20.

two … plus three times four

One pair of parentheses marks the product of three and four. Another encloses the whole calculation, including addition of two.

The inner product is evaluated first. The result is 14.

For a larger expression, relying entirely on parentheses becomes cumbersome and visually confusing. Here is the expression from Section 8 with every operation explicitly grouped:

Every operation parenthesized

The outer parentheses enclose a sum of two products. Inside the second product, a sum encloses a smaller product of five and x.

The nesting is explicit, but following all those parentheses takes work.

Doesn’t that look awful? The parentheses make the grouping precise, but they also crowd the expression and make its structure harder to follow at a glance.

Conventional notation uses the order of operations to imply certain parentheses that we do not write. Multiplication is grouped before addition unless parentheses specify otherwise. With that convention, the two examples from Section 3 become:

two plus three … times four

Only the sum of two and three is enclosed in parentheses. Four sits outside that group as the other factor.

Parentheses explicitly keep the sum together as one factor.

two … plus three times four

The same three numbers appear without parentheses. Multiplication joins the last two as a single term.

The order of operations implicitly keeps the product together as one term.

The expression with x becomes the sum of two products, each with a grouped sum as a factor. Its sums need parentheses, while its products are grouped implicitly by the order of operations.

This inconsistency in how grouping is communicated can cause students trouble: some groups are explicitly marked, while others are left implicit for the student to fill in. Reading the structure requires switching between written grouping marks and remembered conventions.

Exploded Algebra always shows grouping explicitly, in the same way. Horizontal lines extending from plus symbols show the extent of a sum; vertical lines extending from times symbols show the extent of a product. The same lines show those groups whether they stand alone or sit inside a larger expression. There are no alternative grouping conventions to switch between, and no implied parentheses to supply.

“two plus three … times four”

A multiplication bar runs alongside the entire stack of two terms, linking that group to four.

The entire sum is visibly one factor beside 4.

“two … plus three times four”

An addition bar stretches over the entire row of two factors, grouping the row as one term below two.

The entire product is visibly one term below 2.

10. Four first attempts at swapping

Recall that the commutative properties of addition and multiplication allow us to swap the order of two terms in the same sum or two factors in the same product. Consider the following attempts to apply these properties in conventional notation.

Before. Two and x are the first two terms of a three-term sum. Attempted result. The first two terms exchange places. Three stays as the last term.

Before. Two and x are the first two factors of a three-factor product. Attempted result. The first two factors exchange places. Three stays as the last factor.

Before. The first term of the sum is a product, with two before x. Attempted result. X comes first inside the product. The product and the separate term of three stay in place.

Before. Two stands alone as a term. X is a factor in the other term, a product with three. Attempted result. X is now a term on its own, and two is inside the product with three.

At first glance, all four attempts look very similar: each appears to swap the first two elements of the expression. The first three are valid applications of the commutative properties. The fourth, however, is invalid because of an implicit grouping. The order of operations makes x part of the product x multiplied by three, so 2 and x are not two complete terms of the same sum, despite the visual similarity to the other attempts.

Now look at the same four attempts in exploded notation. Each row shows the original expression on the left and the attempted result on the right. The highlighted pieces are the 2 and x being swapped.

Before. Two and x are the first two terms of a three-term sum. The highlights mark two complete terms in the same vertical stack. Attempted result. The first two terms exchange places. Three stays as the last term. The highlights mark two complete terms in the same vertical stack.

Before. Two and x are the first two factors of a three-factor product. The highlights mark two complete factors in the same horizontal row. Attempted result. The first two factors exchange places. Three stays as the last factor. The highlights mark two complete factors in the same horizontal row.

Before. The first term of the sum is a product, with two before x. Both highlights are inside one product row above the separate term of three. Attempted result. X comes first inside the product. The product and the separate term of three stay in place. Both highlights are inside one product row above the separate term of three.

Before. Two stands alone as a term. X is a factor in the other term, a product with three. One highlight covers the complete top term. The other covers only one factor of the lower product row. Attempted result. X is now a term on its own, and two is inside the product with three. One highlight covers the complete top term. The other covers only one factor of the lower product row.

In the first three examples, the highlighted pieces are visibly equal members of one operation: two terms in the same vertical sum or two factors in the same horizontal product. Swapping them preserves that structure. In the fourth, 2 is an entire term of the sum, while x is only one factor within another term. They do not occupy equivalent positions in one operation. Exploded notation makes a visual distinction between the three valid swaps and the invalid one.

11. When a familiar pattern hides the grouping

One response to the inconsistency discussed in Section 9 is that students can get used to it. The conventions are stable: once students learn which groupings are explicit and which are implicit, they should be able to work with them. In that sense, it is a consistent inconsistency.

But a familiar pattern can teach a misleading lesson about what an algebraic operation actually does. Distribution is a useful example. Students repeatedly see a factor outside parentheses distributed across the terms inside them, followed by an answer with no parentheses. It is easy to come away with the idea that distributing means getting rid of parentheses—and therefore getting rid of grouping.

A familiar starting expression

Parentheses around the sum show what the factor of two multiplies.

Multiply 2 by the entire sum of x and 3.

After distributing 2

Two products are separated by a plus sign. Each begins with a copy of the distributed factor of two.

Each product is now grouped implicitly by the order of operations.

The parentheses disappear from the written expression, but grouping has not disappeared. Distribution replaces a product containing a sum with a sum of products. The products in the result still need to be grouped; conventional notation lets the order of operations communicate that grouping implicitly. The repeated disappearance of parentheses can obscure this change in structure.

Correct: distribute 2 and preserve the outside multiplication

Incorrect: remove all the parentheses and leave 4 at the end

This changes the value. If x is 1, the original expression gives 32, while the incorrect expression gives 26. To remove the remaining parentheses correctly, we must also distribute the 4 across the entire sum:

Correct: distribute the remaining factor 4

Each of the two products now ends with a factor of four. The plus sign separates the products.

Both terms receive the factor 4. The expression simplifies to the sum of eight copies of x and twenty-four.

The difficulty comes from the very consistency of the earlier examples: distribution kept producing an expression without parentheses. That visible pattern can become a substitute for understanding the property. When another outside factor appears, the familiar instruction no longer captures what must happen to the grouping.

In exploded notation, the grouping stays visible before and after distributing 2. The new sum is still one complete factor beside 4. Its addition bar makes the extent of that sum explicit, so dropping the grouping would visibly change which parts the 4 multiplies.

When grouping is glossed over, learning algebra can become memorizing what expressions are supposed to look like in particular cases. Frustration follows when an expression looks a little different and the familiar pattern no longer makes clear how to apply the rule. Making the grouping explicit throughout a transformation helps us explain the property itself and carry it into unfamiliar cases.

12. Negatives and the negative unit

A negative sign can be understood as multiplication by the negative unit, negative one. Multiplying a quantity by negative one changes its sign while preserving its size: 5 becomes negative five, and x becomes negative x. This interpretation makes a negative sign an algebraic operation rather than a decoration attached to a symbol.

In exploded notation, negative one appears as an ordinary factor. The horizontal product row makes its relationship to the rest of the expression explicit. A negative number such as negative five is the product formed by multiplying negative one and five, and the expression negative x is the product formed by multiplying negative one and x.

“Negative five is negative one times five.”

The negative unit and five sit in the same horizontal product row.

The factor negative one changes the sign of 5, producing negative five.

“Negative x is negative one times x.”

The negative unit and x are separated by a multiplication bar, showing the sign change as a product.

The factor negative one changes the sign of whatever value x has.

Subtraction can then be read as addition of a negative quantity. The expression x decreased by three means the sum of x and negative three. This does not change the calculation; it expresses subtraction using the two operations already visible in exploded notation—addition and multiplication.

Conventional subtraction

The minus sign between x and three indicates subtraction.

Subtract 3 from x.

“Add x to negative one times three.”

The top term is x. A horizontal addition bar separates it from the lower product of negative one and three.

The second term is negative three, so the sum is x decreased by three.

Because negative one is a factor, the distributive property applies to it just as it does to any other factor. Multiplying an entire sum by negative one changes the sign of every term. Thus the opposite of the entire sum of x and three becomes the sum of the opposites of x and three: one copy of negative one is distributed to x and another copy is distributed to 3.

“Change the sign of the entire sum x plus 3.”

A multiplication bar links negative one to a vertical sum containing both x and three.

The complete sum is one factor beside negative one.

“Distribute negative one to both terms.”

The sum now has two product rows, each with its own factor of negative one.

Both terms change sign, giving the sum of the opposites of x and three.

Two sign changes undo one another. Since the product of two negative units equals one, multiplying by two negative units has the same effect as multiplying by 1. In a longer product such as the product of two negative units and x, the two negative units become 1, and the identity factor 1 can then be removed, leaving x.

13. Exponents as repeated multiplication

A positive whole-number exponent is a compact instruction to use the same base as a factor repeatedly. For example, the fourth power of two means the product of four copies of two, not the product of two and four. The exponent tells us how many copies of the base appear in the product.

Exploded notation displays those copies directly on one horizontal multiplication row. This introduction therefore writes positive integer powers as repeated products, while the conventional expression shown beside each example uses familiar exponent notation.

Conventional power notation

The four is written as a superscript on the base two.

Four copies of 2 are multiplied.

“Multiply four copies of two.”

Four identical factors of two sit in one horizontal product row.

The repeated product has value 16.

The base can be a variable. The expression x raised to the third power means the product of three copies of x. We cannot combine those factors into a number until x is known, but the repeated product still makes the structure of the power clear.

A variable raised to a power

The three is written as a superscript on the variable x.

The base x occurs three times as a factor.

“Multiply three copies of x.”

Three copies of x appear side by side with multiplication bars between them.

Each x is a complete factor in the same product.

An entire grouped expression can also be the base. In the square of the entire sum of x and two, the exponent applies to the complete sum formed by adding x and two. It means the product of two copies of that entire sum, not a sum in which only the middle two entries form a product. Exploded notation shows each complete vertical sum occupying one factor position in the surrounding horizontal product.

A grouped base

The superscript two is outside the parentheses, so it applies to the whole sum.

The complete sum is the base of the power.

“Multiply two copies of the sum x plus 2.”

Two identical vertical sums sit side by side. The multiplication bar reaches across their full height.

Each whole sum is one factor.

A first power contains one copy of the base, so the first power of A equals A. For a nonzero base, the zero power is defined as 1, so the zero power of A equals one when A is nonzero. Negative integer powers involve the reciprocal of a repeated product; that idea becomes clearer after introducing the inverse operator in the next section.

14. Division and the inverse operator

The multiplicative inverse of a nonzero expression A is the quantity that multiplies A to make 1. It is written one divided by A in conventional fraction notation. Exploded notation represents taking an inverse as an operator that encloses its complete operand, making the scope of the denominator explicit.

The restriction that A be nonzero matters: no number multiplied by 0 can produce 1, so zero has no multiplicative inverse. Any cancellation involving an expression and its inverse is valid only where that expression is nonzero.

Conventional reciprocal

One is the numerator and x is the entire denominator.

The denominator is x, with the restriction that x be nonzero.

“Take the inverse of x.”

The inverse symbol surrounds the single variable x.

The inverse operator encloses its complete operand x.

Division by a nonzero expression is multiplication by its inverse. Thus the quotient of A and B and the product of A and the reciprocal of B describe the same operation when B is nonzero. Treating division this way lets products and quotients share one visible structure: the divisor appears as an inverse factor.

Division in conventional notation

The division slash separates the dividend a from the divisor b.

Divide a by b, where b is nonzero.

“Multiply a by the inverse of b.”

The inverse symbol encloses b. The resulting group sits to the right of a on one product row.

The inverse of b is one complete factor beside a.

The operand of the inverse may itself be a sum or product. In the reciprocal of the entire sum of x and three, the entire sum formed by adding x and three is the denominator. Inverting only the 3 would create a different expression. The exploded diagram encloses the complete vertical sum inside one inverse operator, so its scope does not depend on remembering a fraction bar’s grouping role.

A complete sum in the denominator

Parentheses keep the entire sum of x and three together as the denominator.

The reciprocal is defined when the sum of x and three is nonzero.

“Take the inverse of the complete sum x plus 3.”

The inverse symbol encloses the entire vertical sum, including its addition bar.

Both terms belong to the inverse’s operand.

Finally, a nonzero expression multiplied by its own inverse equals 1. In the product of x and its reciprocal, the two factors cancel when x is nonzero. This is the multiplicative counterpart of adding opposite quantities to obtain 0, and it is the basic simplification behind canceling common nonzero factors in rational expressions.

15. Building fluency with conventional notation

  • Explain how students continue to see conventional notation during practice and are required to read and work with it in relation to the exploded representation.
  • Show how students connect each manipulation in exploded notation to the corresponding conventional step, identifying the complete terms, factors, and groups involved.
  • Describe the transition toward reading the grouping and applying algebraic properties directly in conventional notation, with less need for an explicit exploded diagram.
  • State the goal: students use and think about conventional notation correctly, eventually rarely or never needing to return explicitly to exploded notation.
